# Four Months Is the Right Amount of Time to Want Something

I keep a list of things I want. I have kept versions of it since I was nineteen, in notebooks, in the Notes app, once on the back of a receipt I found again two years later in a coat pocket. The receipt one was the most honest, because I wrote it assuming nobody would ever read it, including me.

That turns out to be the whole trick, and it took me an embarrassingly long time to notice.

## The problem with saying it out loud

Manifestation has a branding problem. The word arrives pre-loaded with vision boards made of magazine cutouts, a certain tone of voice, and the implication that the universe is a vending machine that accepts belief as currency. That framing is easy to mock, and people mock it, and then they throw out the part that actually does something.

Here is what I think is actually going on, stripped of the metaphysics.

Wanting something specific is a skill. Most people are bad at it, not because they lack desire but because desire in its raw form is vague and slightly humiliating. "I want to be respected at work" is not a goal, it is a weather system. And the moment you try to say it to another person, you sand it down. You make it reasonable. You make it defensible. You turn *I want to stop feeling small in meetings* into *I'd like to grow into more of a leadership role*, which is a sentence that has never once changed anyone's Tuesday.

Privacy is not a nice-to-have for this. It is the mechanism. You can only be specific about what you want if nobody is watching you want it.

## Why I stopped writing five-year plans

The other failure mode is the horizon.

Five-year plans are fiction. I do not mean that dismissively — I mean they are literally a genre of imaginative writing. You cannot model your own life five years out with any accuracy, so what you produce is a character sketch of a person you would like to be, and characters are not accountable to anyone.

New Year's resolutions have the opposite problem. They are a single arbitrary date with twelve months of runway, which means January is for enthusiasm and February is for quietly not mentioning it again.

Four months is different. Four months is one season plus a little. It is long enough that a real thing can change — you can get meaningfully stronger, you can learn a skill badly, you can leave a job, you can repair or end a relationship. And it is short enough that you can still picture the specific week it happens in. You can feel it. There is no comfortable fog to hide in.

When I set a four-month horizon, the vague stuff falls away on its own, because vague things cannot be scheduled.

## Writing it as though it already happened

This is the part that sounds the most like woo and has the most boring explanation behind it.

Instead of writing *I hope to have finished the thing by December*, you write from December: *I finished it in the second week of November. The last stretch was worse than I expected. I did it anyway.*

Past tense. Details you could not possibly know. Including the parts that went badly.

There is a small body of decision research on this, and it is not about wishes coming true. In the late eighties, Mitchell, Russo, and Pennington ran studies on what they called prospective hindsight: telling people to imagine an outcome had already occurred, then asking them to explain why. People generated substantially more concrete reasons that way than when asked to predict the same outcome in the future tense. Gary Klein later turned the pessimistic version of this into the "premortem," now a standard project-management move — assume the project failed, now tell me what killed it.

The mechanism is unglamorous. Your brain is much better at explaining than at forecasting. Ask it to predict and it produces a shrug. Tell it the thing already happened and it will start filling in the causal chain, and the causal chain is the useful part, because the causal chain is made of things you could actually do on Thursday.

So the letter from four months ahead is not a spell. It is a way of tricking an explanation engine into doing planning work it would otherwise refuse.

Write the bad parts in. *It took longer than I wanted. I almost quit in October.* You are not jinxing anything. You are building something you can act on.

## The gap between a wish and a Thursday

Here is where most of this falls apart, mine included.

You write the beautiful letter. You feel a hit of something. You close the tab. Four months pass. You reread it and feel slightly nauseated.

The letter is the destination. It cannot get you anywhere by itself. What closes the gap is the least romantic object in the entire practice: a list of small, checkable steps, each one boring enough that you could do it while tired.

Not "become healthier." *Walk on Tuesday and Thursday.* Not "grow my network." *Message two people this week.*

The test I use: could a moderately unmotivated version of me do this on a bad day? If no, it is not a step yet, it is still a wish wearing a step's clothes.

And crucially, these live next to the letter. Not in a separate productivity system where they become a chore detached from any reason. The steps only make sense in the light of the thing they are for, and the thing they are for is only real if it has steps.

## On looking at it

The last piece is repetition, and this is the one I resisted longest because it feels the most superstitious.

You have to actually look at the thing. Regularly. Not once in a burst of New Year energy.

There is no magic in the looking. What happens is more mundane: what you look at often stays available to you. It shapes what you notice, what you count as an opportunity, which invitations feel relevant. If you have not thought about a want in six weeks, you will walk straight past the thing that would have served it, because you were not carrying the shape of it around.

This is the honest case for a vision board, and it has nothing to do with attracting anything. It is a memory aid. Images work better than sentences for this because you can absorb an image in half a second, and half a second is all the attention you will reliably give it on a Wednesday morning.

Counting also helps, which surprised me. Knowing that I have returned to something eleven times tells me something true about how much I actually want it. The things I never revisit were never really wants. They were moods I had once. Letting them fade is not failure, it is data.

## What I ended up building

I made myself a thing for this, eventually, because my system was three apps and a receipt.

It is a night sky. Each intention is a star. Ones in the same area of life connect into a constellation. When something actually happens, the star shoots across the sky and moves to a separate view of things that came true, which is the single most useful screen in it, because the default human setting is to forget every want you ever satisfied and feel permanently behind.

There is a letter dated four months out. There is a list of steps. There is a board of images and a count of how many are on it.

The most important design decision was not visual. Everything in it is private by default, enforced at the database level rather than by a promise in a footer — my entries are readable only by my own account, and that is a rule the storage layer applies, not a policy I trust someone to honor. That is not a feature I added for other people. It is the thing that makes me willing to write down the stuff I would not say out loud, which, as established, is the only stuff worth writing down.

## The unglamorous summary

Strip away the vocabulary and what remains is this:

Want something specific. Write it privately enough to be honest. Put it four months out, near enough to picture. Describe it as already done so your brain produces reasons instead of shrugs. Break it into steps a tired person could do. Look at it often. Notice what you keep returning to, and let the rest go.

None of that requires believing the universe is listening.

It only requires that you are.
